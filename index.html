About

Who am I?

Hi — I'm Shiva, a geospatial AI/ML engineer in Albuquerque. I build machine learning systems that understand physical space: what the ground looks like, where things are, and what's moving around them. Satellites, elevation models, LiDAR, maps. If it has a coordinate attached, I've probably spent a weekend arguing with it.

Five years in, that's turned into a fairly specific shape — someone who can build & train models and write the CUDA kernel underneath them and keep the pipeline standing at ten terabytes. Not because I planned it that way, but because in geospatial work those three things keep turning out to be the same problem.

What I've built

Right now I'm at KiloNewton, building terrain intelligence for utility-scale solar. The piece I'm proudest of: our terrain analysis used to be an overnight batch job, so nobody could iterate on a site design in a single working day. I rebuilt it with graph neural networks over TIN meshes and custom CUDA kernels and got a 70 MW site down to under 20 minutes. Same week, different problem — classifying buildable land from raw DEM and shapefiles, which pushed site-suitability accuracy up 35%. Then I built the Mapbox and Kepler.gl interface so the layout team could run it without me in the loop.

Before that I spent two years at Discover Partners Institute doing spatio-temporal modelling across 30 public health studies, alongside my master's at UIC. That's where I learned to build things that hold up under scrutiny — Optuna search, A/B validation frameworks, PySpark and BigQuery pipelines pulling 500+ heterogeneous sources together at 99.2% spatial accuracy. Forecast accuracy went up 23%; more usefully, iteration time dropped 40%, which is what actually lets a research team move.

And I started at Justdial in Bengaluru on location search — 10M+ monthly users, PostGIS queries and caching that took inference from 100 ms to 10 ms under 50,000 concurrent load. That job is why I still think in latency budgets before I think about architecture.

How I work

I start at the end. Before touching a model I want to know who reads the output, what they do with it, and how wrong it's allowed to be — because that determines everything upstream, from architecture to how I split the validation folds.

Then I go for the boring bottleneck first. Usually it isn't the model. It's the six-hour runtime, the projection mismatch nobody caught, the pipeline that silently drops a tile. I'd rather ship a straightforward model on solid ground than a clever one on sand — and I benchmark against a simple baseline every time, partly out of honesty, partly because the baseline wins more often than people admit.

And I try to hand things over properly. Documentation, a working interface, a repo someone else can pick up. Work that only functions while I'm holding it isn't finished.

I've shipped all of them, which makes me most useful on the seams where they meet — the model that needs a GPU rewrite, the pipeline quietly corrupting a projection, the score nobody can defend. Lately I've been going deeper on geospatial foundation models and the cloud-native stack the field is consolidating around, and I'd like my next role to put that into production.

I'm on an H-1B in Albuquerque and open to relocating. If this sounds like your team, I'd like to hear about it.

The origin story

It started with maps.

Long before I wrote a line of Python I was the person who'd pull up a route and actually study it — not just to get somewhere, but because a map is a compressed argument about the world. This road exists. This one is faster at 6pm. The river is why the road bends here. You're looking at thousands of decisions someone made about physical space, flattened into something you can read in a second.

If a map can tell me how to reach a destination, what else could it tell me — if something were reading it properly?

That's the thread I've been pulling ever since. First the geometry itself: projections, elevation, why a two-degree slope changes what you can build on a hillside. Then the machine learning on top of it — segmentation over satellite imagery, graph networks over terrain meshes, forecasting across space and time. Somewhere in there GIS stopped being a mapping discipline to me and became a modelling one: the map wasn't the output anymore, it was the input.

What I care about now is the last step — turning all of it back into something a person can actually use. A model that knows a hillside is unbuildable does nothing until it reaches the engineer deciding where the panels go. A route is worthless until it's in front of a driver. That's why I keep ending up building the interface too, and why I care as much about a six-hour runtime as an accuracy score.

Spatial data only gets interesting to me at the point where it becomes a decision somebody makes.
